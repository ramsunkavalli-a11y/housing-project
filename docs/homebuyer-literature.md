# Literature and methodological evidence

Sweep date: **2026-09-27**. Scope: U.S. homebuyer relevance, residential choice, spatial measurement, accessibility, and practical public data. This is a targeted narrative sweep, not a systematic review, meta-analysis, or exhaustive ranking of all buyer preferences.

Search families included residential location choice/location variables; stated versus revealed preferences; homebuyer neighborhood features; accessibility/network measures; geographic crosswalks and ACS uncertainty; risk information and housing; and official documentation for each inventory component. Prioritized research papers, author/institution repositories, survey producers, and data publishers. Excluded commercial “best neighborhood” rankings as evidence of quality. No paywalled full report was purchased.

**Evidence types differ:** buyer surveys reveal stated priorities; observed moves reflect constraints as well as preferences; experiments can identify effects within their setting; methods papers explain measurement; dataset documentation establishes what a field means. None supplies a validated set of weights for this product.

## Product conclusions supported by this sweep

**Strong basis for collecting:** housing feasibility, actual destination access, daily services, physical surroundings, and clearly explained risks. **Strong basis for careful methods:** heterogeneous preferences, imperfect data, uncertainty, geography alignment, and separating individual requirements from population averages.

**Not established:** an optimal number of questions; one national definition of a neighborhood; validated weights; a scientifically justified “overall fit” score; current free listing coverage; or evidence that more collected variables always produce better recommendations.

The recommendation to keep a broad inventory and narrow it using feature-group tests and buyer feedback is our synthesis. It should be tested, not described as a result proved by these papers.

## How to narrow after collecting a pilot sample

For each feature family, document a concrete buyer decision it could change, then check incremental value, coverage, interpretability, update cost, and error consequences. Keep hard requirements separate from preferences. Compare results with and without redundant feature groups. Preserve an unknown rather than eliminate an area based on weak evidence.

Do not optimize only for the majority survey respondent. A rarely requested feature can be essential to a smaller group, such as step-free access or a particular care destination. Conversely, a frequently mentioned aspiration may be impossible to measure nationally and should remain a verification question.

Future research should include actual home seekers, smaller towns and rural settings, multi-person disagreements, mobility limitations, changing household needs, and the usefulness of cross-street starting points. These remain evaluation gaps, not completed research.
